How to Buy a Government Surplus Vehicle

By Deal1Cars · Published Nov 25, 2026

Federal agencies sell vehicles they no longer need, and the public can bid on them. Here is the process from sign-up to driving home, with the points that trip up first-time buyers. Details below come from GSA's own buyer FAQ, and each sale has its own terms, so always read the item page for the vehicle you want.

Short version: register for free, read the sale terms, inspect if you can, set a maximum price that includes taxes and transport, pay by credit card or wire, and be ready to pick the vehicle up yourself.

1. Register before you want to bid

Anyone can browse GSA Auctions, but you must register and log in to bid. Registration is open to individuals and companies, and an individual can have only one account per Social Security Number. GSA verifies your identity through a credit bureau. Giving a credit card at sign-up is optional, and GSA says it is not charged or stored. Register a day or two early, because a failed identity check means a manual review.

2. Find a vehicle

Use the Deal1Cars search to filter GSA and eBay Motors listings by type, state, price or distance from your ZIP code, then open the listing on the original site. Government vehicles are usually sold where they sit, so distance matters, since you will have to go and get it.

3. Read the item page and sale terms

This is where the real rules are. Look for the vehicle's location, inspection dates, bid deposit rules, payment methods, the removal deadline, and whether there is a reserve price. If a reserve is not met, you are not awarded the item. Deadlines for payment and removal are set in the sale terms and in your award email, not in a general rule, so write them down.

4. Inspect it, and treat it as sold as-is

Government surplus is normally sold in the condition it is in, with no warranty. If the item page lists inspection times, go. If you cannot, at least run the VIN through a history report, read every line of the description, and study every photo. Look for the keys, the title status and the mileage in the listing. Missing information is a reason to bid less, not a reason to assume the best.

5. Decide your maximum before you start

Work out your top price including state sales or use tax, transport, repairs and registration (see our guide to auction fees and hidden costs). GSA's site offers two bid types: a proxy bid, where you set a maximum and the system bids for you in increments, and a flat bid, which is a manual bid that must meet the increment. You can lower a bid but not cancel it. Closing times can also extend if bidding continues or there are technical problems, so do not plan around the final second.

6. Pay on time

For personal property, GSA says it currently accepts only credit cards and wire transfers, with no cash, personal checks, cashier's checks or money orders. Credit cards are limited to $24,999.99 per transaction and no more than two cards per transaction. Larger purchases usually need a wire. Missing the payment deadline has a price: GSA lists default fees of the full award amount for purchases under $325, $325 for purchases up to $100,000, and 5% of the award amount above that.

7. Arrange pickup yourself

GSA does not ship purchases. You are responsible for loading and removing the vehicle by the deadline in your award email, and you should contact the location first to confirm when and how you can get in. If someone else collects it for you, they will need a letter of authorization, photo ID and the signed receipt and release form. For a car that cannot be driven, price a tow or a trailer before you bid.

8. Title, registration and taxes

GSA says a vehicle title is issued in your business name only if you registered as a company, and it cannot be changed after the award, so register under the name that will own the vehicle. GSA also says it does not charge sales tax or a buyer's premium, but you may owe state sales or use tax and registration fees when you title the vehicle, so ask your state's motor vehicle agency what to expect.

Common mistakes

  • Bidding without reading the terms. Removal and payment deadlines are strict.
  • Forgetting the cost of getting it home. A cheap truck 1,500 miles away is not cheap.
  • Assuming it runs. Check the listing for whether it is a running vehicle, and expect to do some work.
  • Waiting for the last seconds. Late bids can be rejected, and close times can move.
  • Registering at the last minute. Identity checks can take time.
